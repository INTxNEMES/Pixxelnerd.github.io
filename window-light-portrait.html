How to shoot a good portrait using just a window

13 Nov 2026

You don’t need a studio or lighting gear to take portraits that look professional. A window, some patience and a few small adjustments will do it. This works with a phone or a camera.

What you need

  • A window with daylight coming through, but not direct sun
  • A plain wall or simple background
  • A sheet of white paper, foam board or light cloth
  • A willing subject, or yourself with a timer

Step 1: Find the right window

Look for a window that gets bright light but no direct sunbeams. A north-facing window is ideal because its light stays soft all day. If the sun is shining straight in, hang a thin white curtain or tracing paper over the glass.

Turn off the room lights. Mixing indoor bulbs with daylight gives skin an orange or green tint, and the photo looks off even if everything else is right.

Step 2: Position your subject

Have your subject stand or sit about a metre from the window, turned roughly 45 degrees to it. One side of the face is lit and the other falls into gentle shadow, which gives the face shape and depth. Facing the window directly makes the light flat. Standing side-on makes the shadow side too dark.

Step 3: Fill the shadows

Hold your white board on the shadow side of the face, just out of frame, and angle it to bounce window light back. Closer gives a brighter fill, further away a subtler one. This is the biggest single improvement you can make.

Step 4: Set up your camera

On a phone, tap the face to focus, then slide the exposure down a little. Phones tend to overexpose faces against a bright window, and it’s easier to keep highlights than to recover them later.

On a camera, use a wide aperture such as f/2 to f/2.8 for a soft background, and keep the shutter speed above 1/125 to avoid blur. Raise the ISO if the room is dim. Hold the camera at eye level or slightly above, since shooting from below is rarely flattering.

Step 5: Direct the pose without making it stiff

Most people freeze when a camera points at them. A few prompts help:

  • Ask them to look out of the window, then slowly turn towards you.
  • Have them breathe in, let it out, and shoot as they relax.
  • Give their hands something to do, like holding a cup.

Take more frames than you think you need. The best expression is often the one just after they stop posing.

Step 6: Check before you finish

Are the eyes sharp? Is there a bright reflection of the window in them? That small highlight makes a face look alive. Is anything distracting in the background, like a plug socket or clutter? Fixing these takes seconds while your subject is still there.

Common mistakes

Shooting into the window. The face goes dark and the window blows out. Keep the window beside you or behind you, not behind the subject.

Using flash. It flattens everything and ruins the soft light you just set up.

Overediting. A small exposure boost and a slight warmth adjustment is usually enough.

Try it this week

Pick one window, one person and fifteen minutes. Take twenty photos, change one thing between sets (the angle, the distance or the reflector), and compare them side by side. That comparison will teach you more about light than any list of tips.
